---
title: Do intelligent systems reuse the same shape for different concepts?
---
Some concepts seem to share the same *structure*, even though they have nothing to do with each other.

Take years. Each decade can be thought of as wrapping around, but each century sits one level higher than the last, so going from the 1900s to the 2000s is less like a straight line and more like climbing a helix. Language models seem to represent years this way: Bhalla et al. (2026) find a helix for years in Llama's residual stream, a circle-like structure for days of the week (see also Engels et al., 2025), and a paraboloid for colors.

So how about pitch/tonality? Pitch classes wrap around (C, C#, ..., B, back to C), with each octave sitting one level higher than the last (Shepard, 1982).

If a system already has a "helix" for years, does it build a separate helix for pitch, or does it reuse the one it already has? Reusing it would be the economical choice. Why learn the same shape twice?

Using sparse autoencoders (SAEs), Bhalla et al. (2026) break a model's activations into a dictionary of directions, which they call "atoms," and only let a handful of these atoms (*k* of them) be active for any given input. In their synthetic experiments, when *k* is very small, the same atoms end up shared across different manifolds. When *k* is moderate, compact groups of atoms emerge that capture individual manifolds.

That sharing could be a system reusing a shape, or it could just be an artifact of forcing the SAE to describe everything with too few atoms. I don't know how you'd tell those two apart yet.

On another note, I'm even curious about manifolds that aren't homeomorphic (you can't continuously deform one into the other) but are still 'similar' in that a small change of a feature can get you from one to the other. For example, a torus and a Möbius strip (Sanderson, 2024; Seipel, 2024). Could concept manifolds be reused and manipulated to serve other concepts?

*shrug*
