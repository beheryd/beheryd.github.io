---
title: Do intelligent systems reuse the same shape for different concepts?
---
A random thought after a talk I heard yesterday: some concepts seem to share the same *structure*, even though they have nothing to do with each other.

Take years. Each decade wraps around, but each century sits one level higher than the last, so going from the 1900s to the 2000s is less like a straight line and more like climbing a helix. Language models seem to represent years this way: Bhalla et al. (2026) find a helix for years in Llama's residual stream, next to a circle for days of the week (see also Engels et al., 2025) and a paraboloid for colors.

Now take musical pitch. Pitch classes wrap around (C, C#, ..., B, back to C), and each octave sits one level higher than the last. People have described that as a helix for decades (Shepard, 1982).

So here's my question: if a system already has a "helix" for years, does it build a separate helix for pitch, or does it reuse the one it already has? Reusing it would be the economical choice. Why learn the same shape twice?

There's a hint of this in the paper. When the SAE is pushed toward denser codes, some atoms end up shared across different manifolds. That could be the model reusing a shape, or it could just be an artifact of how the SAE carves things up. I don't know how you'd tell those two apart yet.

If the answer is "yes, the shape gets reused," that would be pretty nifty. It would mean the basic unit of a concept isn't the content but the geometry, and content just gets mapped onto whatever shape fits.
