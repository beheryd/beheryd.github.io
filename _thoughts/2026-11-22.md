---
title: How do we know we'd like a combination?
---
"What would you like to order today?"<br>
"Could I have a deep dish?"

"Would you like olives with that?"<br>
"Oh! I've never had them on deep dish before, but that sounds good!"

"How about basbousa?"<br>
"Ew! I like basbousa, but not on pizza!"

How do we know whether we'll like something without ever trying it? I like deep dish and I like basbousa, but I can tell you right away that I wouldn't like them together. Somehow, we can *compose* our preferences: we can predict how they'd change for combinations we've never experienced.

How should we think about preferences in general? If preferences only came from past experiences and the reward attached to them, I'd have no opinion on basbousa deep dish pizza.

When people imagine a new experience they've never had, the brain seems to build its value by pulling up the memories of its parts at the same time, in the hippocampus and medial prefrontal cortex (Barron et al., 2013).

How does the mind (and thus the brain) infer value from novel compositions?
