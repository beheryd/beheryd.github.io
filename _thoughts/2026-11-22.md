---
title: How do we know we'd hate cotton candy on pizza?
---
"What would you like to order today?"
"Could I have a deep dish?"

"Would you like olives with that?"
"Oh! I've never had them on deep dish before, but that sounds good!"

"How about cotton candy?"
"Ew! I like cotton candy, but not on pizza!"

How do we know whether we'll like something without ever trying it? I like deep dish and I like cotton candy, but I can tell you right away that I wouldn't like them together. Somehow we can *compose* our preferences: we predict how they'd change for combinations we've never experienced.

This makes me wonder how we should think about preferences in general. If preferences only came from past experiences and the reward attached to them, I'd have no opinion on cotton candy pizza. So something else has to be learned too: some model of how foods relate to each other, and to our tastebuds (salty goes with savory, sugary fluff doesn't go with melted cheese).

There's some evidence for this. When people imagine a new experience they've never had, the brain seems to build its value by pulling up the memories of its parts at the same time, in the hippocampus and medial prefrontal cortex (Barron et al., 2013). And at the scale of whole cuisines, which ingredients get paired together seems to follow how many flavor compounds they share, at least in Western cooking (Ahn et al., 2011).

But that still leaves my question open. What does that "model of how foods go together" actually look like, and where does it come from?
